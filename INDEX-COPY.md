# DEAFJOBS — Index Page Copy

Short-form copy for the split front page, sized for mobile. Green half speaks to Deaf and hard-of-hearing candidates; blue half speaks to recruiters and hiring managers.

**Design note:** the wordmark straddling the divide is doing real work — it says "two sides meeting" without a word of explanation. Both halves follow the same shape: a bold lead line, two or three short lines, a bold closing line. Read the two closing lines together and they mirror — *the job you actually want* / *post a role, answer four questions*.

## Copy principles for this page

Worth writing down, because these are easy to lose when someone else edits the page later.

**1. No traits, no inspiration.**
Don't sell "Deaf people are patient / focused / loyal." Positive stereotypes are still stereotypes, and this product exists because Deaf people get boxed in by other people's assumptions. A manager who hires expecting a patient employee and gets a normal human with a normal range of moods feels misled — and that's the hire that doesn't stick.

Sell instead: **facts they can check and costs they can budget.** Those can't be disproven by one bad Tuesday.

**2. Claims about willingness are fine. Claims about personality are not.**
"They've done this more times than you have" describes a *stance* — meeting you halfway, with practice. Honest and verifiable. "Deaf people are patient" describes a trait. Keep the first, never write the second.

**3. Claims about the team beat claims about the person.**
"Teams that hire Deaf employees end up communicating better with each other" flatters the manager's team instead of flattening the candidate. Prefer this shape.

**4. Hedges make it stronger, not weaker.**
"*Some* will surprise you" is more credible than "Deaf employees are exceptional." Overclaiming is what makes a hiring manager stop believing the rest of the page.

**5. The law is a floor, not a pitch.**
Compliance framing makes people defensive and buys a grudging hire. The PRD's Goal 3 is *genuine confidence, not just compliance*. Mention the legal framework only as reassurance in the footnote, never as the argument. Lead with the tax credit (a carrot) over the statute (a stick).

**6. Answer the fear, not the objection.**
The stated objection is cost. The actual fear is *this will all be on me, forever.* The strong lines all answer that: the support is bounded, the learning curve ends, and the candidate is carrying half of it.

**7. On mobile, the whole argument is four lines.**
If a new line doesn't survive being read on a phone in three seconds, it belongs on a deeper page, not here.

## To verify before this goes live

- **What "comes with your posting" covers.** The page promises captioning is included; make sure the posting flow delivers it and that the duration is stated where the employer sees it before paying.
- **Federal tax credits.** Confirm current provisions and eligibility with an accountant before making the claim in the footnote.
- Both are shown to employers as fact. Getting either wrong costs more credibility than leaving it out would.
